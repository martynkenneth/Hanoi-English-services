# Dread Signal: Script Kit

Built from the structure of PsychToons' *Psychology of Addiction* (which you liked for its pacing and visuals). We borrow the **method**, not their art, character or name.

---

## 1. The formula (10–12 min, about 1,500–1,800 words, spoken at about 150 wpm)

| # | Beat | Time | What it does | Visual rule |
|---|------|------|--------------|-------------|
| 1 | **Cold open "you" scene** | 0:00–0:40 | Put the viewer inside a moment they've lived. Ends on something that "doesn't add up." | One figure, one room, almost no motion. |
| 2 | **The paradox, stated** | 0:40–1:15 | Say the strange truth out loud in one sentence. Then: "Most people assume X. It's the opposite." | Text card with the key line. |
| 3 | **Channel intro + promise** | 1:15–1:40 | "Welcome to Dread Signal. Today: what's really happening, and what you can do about it." | Logo sting, 2 sec max. |
| 4 | **The research, in plain words** | 1:40–3:30 | One landmark study: who, when, what they did, what they found. Name the researchers. | Simple diagram: two lines, two boxes, an arrow. |
| 5 | **The anchor metaphor** | 3:30–4:30 | One image that carries the whole video (their "doorbell"). Callback 3+ times. | The metaphor gets its own recurring drawing. |
| 6 | **The honest caveat** | inside 4 or 6 | "This study gets oversold. Here's the modest version." Builds trust; most channels skip it. | Figure raises a hand or a "?" card. |
| 7 | **The numbered list** | 4:30–8:00 | 3–4 "faces" of the phenomenon. Each one: a short scene, then the mechanism, then **the cost**. | A new mini-scene per item and a number card. |
| 8 | **The way out** | 8:00–10:00 | 3–4 practical moves, each tied to evidence. Work *with* the brain, not against it. | Before/after split frames. |
| 9 | **Reflective close** | 10:00–11:00 | Zoom out to the human condition. One quote from a historical figure. | Slow, wide, quiet shot. |
| 10 | **Comment prompt + CTA** | last 40s | "Which of the four do you recognise?" Then share/subscribe. | List recap on screen. |

**Voice rules:** short sentences, second person ("you"), one idea per sentence, a pause after every key line, no jargon without a plain-English translation right after it.

**Visual rules:** a new image every 3–6 seconds; each picture shows the literal sentence being spoken; limited palette; lots of empty space. **For Dread Signal, make it your own:** e.g. cool grey-blue and off-white with one warning colour (signal red/amber), slightly grainy, a recurring "signal" motif (a pulsing dot, a static line, a radio tower).

## 2. Topic list (ranked for hook strength and how well the research holds up)

✅ = solid evidence · ⚠️ = popular but contested (still usable if you include the honest caveat)

1. ✅ **The Dread Paradox:** why people choose a bigger pain now over a smaller one later (*sample script below*)
2. ✅ **The Uncertainty Paradox:** knowing it's bad is less stressful than *maybe* bad (de Berker et al., 2016)
3. ✅ **The Bystander Effect:** more witnesses, less help (Darley & Latané, 1968); include the 2019 CCTV update showing people *do* usually intervene
4. ✅ **The Paradox of Choice:** more options, less satisfaction (Schwartz); ⚠️ the jam study replicates weakly, so say so
5. ✅ **The Hedonic Treadmill:** why getting what you want doesn't last
6. ✅ **The Spotlight Effect:** everyone is watching you (they're not) (Gilovich, 2000)
7. ✅ **The Peak-End Rule:** you remember pain by its worst moment and its ending, not its length (Kahneman, cold-water studies)
8. ✅ **Learned Helplessness, reversed:** Maier & Seligman (2016): passivity is the default; *control* is what's learned
9. ✅ **Ironic Process:** "don't think of a white bear" (Wegner, 1987)
10. ✅ **The Illusion of Explanatory Depth:** you can't actually explain how a zipper works (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002)
11. ✅ **The Mere Exposure Effect:** you like things simply because they're familiar (Zajonc)
12. ✅ **Hindsight Bias:** "I knew it all along" (you didn't)
13. ✅ **The Sunk Cost Trap:** why you finish bad films, jobs and relationships
14. ✅ **Doomscrolling and negativity bias:** why bad news grips harder than good
15. ✅ **The Fermi Paradox (psychology angle):** why silence scares us more than an answer
16. ⚠️ **The Dunning–Kruger Effect:** real data, but the famous graph is partly a statistical artifact; great "everyone gets this wrong" episode
17. ⚠️ **The Zeigarnik Effect:** unfinished tasks haunt you; the evidence is mixed
18. ⚠️ **The Stanford Prison Experiment:** what *actually* happened (the exposé angle is the hook)
19. ✅ **The Ben Franklin Effect:** you like people more after doing *them* a favour
20. ✅ **Pluralistic Ignorance:** everyone privately disagrees, so everyone stays silent

## 3. Sample script: *The Dread Paradox: Why Your Brain Would Rather Hurt Now*

**Target:** about 10 min, about 1,550 words. `[V: …]` = visual cue.

### Cold open
[V: dark bedroom, figure lying awake, phone face-down on the nightstand]

It's Sunday night. Nothing bad has happened yet. The meeting is tomorrow at ten. The test results come back on Thursday. The conversation you've been avoiding is still days away.

[V: clock hands spinning slowly]

And yet you can feel it. A low hum in your chest. You haven't been hurt. You're just waiting to be. And somewhere around midnight, a strange thought shows up.

[V: thought bubble: "Just get it over with."]

You'd almost rather it happened *now*. Even if it's worse.

### The paradox
[V: text card: "Waiting can hurt more than the pain itself."]

That's the dread paradox. Most people assume pain is the problem, and that we'd always choose less of it, later. But when researchers actually tested this, a surprising number of people did the opposite. They chose *more* pain, sooner, just to stop waiting for it.

### Intro
[V: Dread Signal logo: a single pulse on a flat line]

Welcome to Dread Signal. Today we're going to look at why your brain treats waiting as its own kind of injury, and what you can do when the countdown starts.

### The research
[V: simple lab: figure in a scanner, a dial labelled VOLTS]

In 2006, a team at Emory University led by neuroscientist Gregory Berns put 32 volunteers in a brain scanner and gave them a choice. They were going to receive electric shocks to the foot. Not dangerous, but unpleasant. They could pick *when* each shock came and, in some trials, *how strong* it was.

[V: two doors. Door A: "small shock, wait 27 seconds". Door B: "bigger shock, now"]

Here's what happened. Almost everyone preferred to get it over with sooner. And a subset, the researchers called them "extreme dreaders," went further. They chose a *stronger* shock right away rather than wait for a weaker one.

[V: brain outline, regions lighting up as a countdown ticks]

The scans showed why. During the wait, the brain areas that respond to pain were already active. The suffering wasn't saved up for the moment of impact. It was being paid in instalments, every second of the countdown.

[V: earlier study card: "Loewenstein, 1987"]

This wasn't a one-off. Back in 1987, economist George Loewenstein asked people how much they'd pay to avoid a shock at different delays. Many would pay more to avoid one a year away than one coming *right now*. Waiting made it worse, not better.

### The anchor metaphor
[V: a taxi meter, ticking]

Think of dread as a taxi meter. The pain itself is the destination. But the meter starts running the moment you know you're going, and it doesn't stop until you arrive. Some people would rather take a shorter, bumpier road than watch that meter climb.

### The honest caveat
[V: figure raising a hand, "?" card]

Now, let's be careful. These were lab shocks, measured in seconds, not real-life dread that lasts weeks. And not everyone was an extreme dreader; most people just mildly preferred "sooner." So the modest version is this: anticipation carries a real cost, and your brain counts it.

### Four faces of dread
[V: number card "1"]

**One: procrastination that looks like laziness.** You avoid the email because opening it starts the meter. But not opening it keeps the meter running all day. The cost: you pay the dread *and* the task.

[V: number card "2"]

**Two: the worst-case rehearsal.** Your mind plays the bad conversation fifty times before it happens once. Each rehearsal is another shock. The cost: one hard moment becomes fifty.

[V: number card "3"]

**Three: self-sabotage.** Some people end a relationship or quit a job *before* it can go wrong, just to make the waiting stop. The cost: you choose the loss to escape the suspense of maybe losing.

[V: number card "4"]

**Four: checking.** Refreshing the inbox, the results page, the read receipt. Every check is a tiny hope of ending the wait, and every empty screen resets the meter. The cost: hours of your life spent at the door.

### The way out
[V: split frame: meter running / meter off]

So what do you do? You can't delete dread; it's an old survival system. But you can stop feeding the meter.

**One: shorten the ride.** Wherever you can, move the hard thing *earlier*. Send the email at 9 a.m., not 5 p.m. Book the appointment for Monday, not Friday. You're not being brave. You're cutting the fare.

**Two: schedule the worry.** Researchers studying worry-postponement have found that giving worry a fixed 15-minute slot each day can reduce how much it leaks into the rest of the day. When it shows up early, tell it: "Not now. 6 p.m."

**Three: make the unknown smaller.** Write down the actual worst case, the most likely case, and one thing you'd do if the worst happened. Dread feeds on fog. A plan turns the fog into a shape.

**Four: stop checking on a schedule.** Decide in advance: "I'll look at 12 and at 5." Each check you skip is a shock you didn't give yourself.

### Reflective close
[V: wide shot, figure standing on a hill at dawn]

Here's the strange comfort in all this. The fact that waiting hurts means your brain is trying to protect you. It just hasn't learned the difference between preparing and suffering.

[V: quote card]

Seneca wrote this nearly two thousand years ago: "We suffer more often in imagination than in reality." He wasn't telling you the bad thing won't come. He was telling you to stop living there before it does.

### CTA
[V: list recap of the four faces]

So which face of dread shows up most for you? Procrastination, rehearsal, self-sabotage, or checking? Tell me in the comments. Naming it turns down the signal. If this helped, share it with someone who's waiting on something right now. And subscribe to Dread Signal. See you next time.
